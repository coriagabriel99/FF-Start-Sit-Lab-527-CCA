Start/Sit Lab: Week 10

Lineups, matchup grades, usage, trades and your whole league in one place. Data as of Monday, Nov. 16. All Week 10 games except Chargers–Ravens (Monday night) are final: those players show actual points (tagged FINAL), and season averages, touches, snaps, recent form, garbage-time splits and defense-vs-position grades now run through Week 10 (nflverse). Week 11 matchups, lines and weather load after Monday night.

How the numbers work

  • Matchup grade (0–100). Half-PPR points each defense allows to that position (through Week 9) versus league average, blended 50/50 with the team’s Vegas implied points this week. 50 is neutral.
  • Projection. Season PPR points per game, adjusted by matchup and game environment (0.4 weight each), then by weather (passing game only: −2% to −4% in wind or rain) and by any role change I could source (for example, the target vacuum left by an injured teammate). The injury switch multiplies by a chance-to-play: questionable 75%, doubtful 20%, limited practice 92%, out 0%.
  • Usage. Snap share and target share: Edgehalla (nflverse), through Week 9. Red-zone and goal-line carries: 4for4, through Week 9. Team dropbacks (attempts plus sacks): NFL.com, through Week 9. Targets per route run and routes are NFL Next Gen Stats through Week 8 (via Fantasy Footballers). Slot vs outside for receivers is the share of receiving fantasy points from each alignment (Razzball); route alignment is not public, so for tight ends it is shown only where it has been reported this season: Isaiah Likely runs about 80% of his routes from the slot and only about 5% inline, Mike Gesicki about 86% slot with no inline routes (RotoBaller, Footballguys). Other tight ends show a dash rather than a guess. Route share and target share are shown only where I found a sourced 2026 number. “Slot share of points” is the share of a receiver’s receiving fantasy points that came from the slot (Razzball, through Week 9). It is a proxy for alignment, not the share of routes run in the slot.
  • Defense type. “Pass-funnel” or “run-funnel” compares what a defense allows to QB/WR/TE versus RB. Man and zone rates are only listed for defenses where I found a 2026 figure.
  • Schedule and trades. Rest-of-season schedule averages how many points each remaining opponent (Weeks 10–17) has allowed to the position versus league average, from the full 2026 schedule. The trade grade projects every player week by week (opponent, bye, injury timeline) and compares your best lineup before and after; the other side is graded on points above a replacement-level starter. Fantasy playoff weeks can be weighted 1.5×. Injury timelines (for example Nacua out through Week 14) come from reported estimates and can change.
  • Scoring. Season figures are PPR. Half-PPR and standard subtract 0.5 or 1 point per catch per game using each player’s catches (exact for about 190 players, estimated from targets for the rest); TE premium adds per catch; 6-point passing touchdowns add 2 points per passing touchdown per game. Replacement levels move with the setting.
  • Waiver wire. Each available player comes with the drop that opens his roster spot: the player whose removal leaves your projected best lineup strongest over the rest of the season while protecting trade assets (each point of market value counts as half a season point, so a cheap bench body goes before a tradeable one), skipping anyone whose game this week is already final. Lists players not on any roster you have entered (or not on yours, when no league is loaded) at a position, next to your own players there, with this week’s projection, season and last-3 averages, usage, snap share, target share or backfield share, rest-of-season points and market value. A row is marked as an upgrade when it beats your weakest player at the position by half a point this week or three points over the rest of the season, and the Why column spells out the matchup rank, the role, the recent trend and any garbage-time inflation for both players.
  • Compare players. A roster-free check on the Trades tab: any players on each side, graded on rest-of-season points over a replacement starter per week (the same grade scale as the trade builder), with a market-value check, each player’s projected Weeks 10–17 points with byes and injury timelines, and a week-by-week comparison of the two sides.
  • Floor and ceiling. From each player’s weekly scores this season: the floor is his 20th-percentile week and the ceiling his 80th, each scaled to this week’s projection. Boom rate is the share of games at 1.5× his average or better; bust rate the share under half. Head-to-head offers a “safest lineup” built on floors and a “highest-ceiling lineup” built on ceilings next to the best-projected one; the options table shows which wins most often against the opponent’s lineup.
  • Bye planner. For each remaining week, your players on bye or still on an injury timeline, whether the rest of the roster fills every slot (Flex and Superflex from the leftovers), and the two best fill-ins at each short position among players nobody in your league has rostered, by that week’s projection.
  • Week recap. For every team, the points its set lineup actually scored (projections stand in for games not yet played) against the best lineup it could have started from its roster in hindsight, the gap left on the bench, and whether the head-to-head result would have flipped with both sides optimal.
  • Accuracy. Rebuilds each player’s pre-game projection from the data as of Week 9 (season average, last-3 form, matchup, line, weather) and compares it with his actual Week 10 score; shows mean error, bias and the share within 5 points by position, plus the biggest misses.
  • Trends. Usage → Trends & compare lists every player’s last six games of snaps, touches or targets and points as small lines, with the change in each between his last three games and the games before, and a Compare panel that overlays any number of players on one chart (points, usage or snaps) with their floor, ceiling, boom and bust rates side by side. Each player’s details list the same six-game numbers.
  • History and awards. Every week moves into history automatically once all of its games are final, using the lineups set in the tool (best-projected for teams without one), and can be edited afterwards. Under League → History & awards you type each week’s starters (and bench) for every team, the same way rosters are entered, and set that week’s matchups; a paste option is there too. Player ranks → By week shows every player’s actual points and usage line for any week. Points come from the real box scores in your scoring (kickers and defenses included). The awards use every week entered: best and worst manager by lineup efficiency (points started as a share of the best lineup available from starters plus bench), luckiest and unluckiest by real wins minus expected wins (each week’s share of the league a team outscored), and most points left on the bench. The records table adds median scoring: a second game each week against the league median, shown on its own and combined with head-to-head as a double-header record.
  • Playoff odds. A Monte Carlo simulation of the remaining regular season (2,000 runs; 1,500 for trade scenarios). Each team’s weekly score is drawn around its projected best lineup for that week (season-adjusted when standings are entered) with the same spread used for win chances; scheduled games are played as entered and unscheduled teams are paired at random each week. Standings seed the wins and points, the top N teams by record then points make it, and the number of spots and the last regular-season week are set under Rosters → Standings.
  • Upgrade a position. Picks the position and the window (rest of season, or fantasy playoffs Weeks 15–17), finds players at that position who raise your projected best lineup over that window, and pairs each with the realistic offer from your roster (market value 85–135% with the consolidation premium and the best-player rule). The week-by-week chart on a graded trade shows the percentage change in your projected points each week and, where your schedule is set, the change in win chance.
  • Locked players. Once a player’s game for the week is final (Thursday night this week), he is left out of every suggestion list by default, since most platforms lock traded players until the week ends; a switch in Suggested trades turns that off, and the trade builder flags any locked player you add by hand.
  • Trade stance. Each leaguemate can be marked Normal, Stingy or Open to deals under Rosters. Stingy managers only see offers that give them at least even market value (a 2-for-1 still carries the 10% premium) and never weaken their projected lineup, and the “favors you” groups skip them entirely; Open managers accept down to 80% of value. Teams whose names contain Cobras, Jasmine, Armando or Brian default to Stingy, as you asked, until you change the setting.
  • Realism rules. Besides market value, every suggestion must pass a best-player check: when one side gets the best player in the deal, the other side’s best piece must be worth at least 60% of him, so a star is never offered for a pile of depth or a one-QB quarterback for a top back. Clearly lopsided deals are no longer listed anywhere; the most one-sided group shown is 80–90% of value back.
  • Market trade value. Trade realism runs on FantasyCalc’s redraft values, which are computed from thousands of real completed trades in 12-team, one-QB, PPR leagues (pulled Nov. 14 from the link you gave me; the top 154 players are loaded). The raw numbers are divided by 100, so Gibbs is 107, Smith-Njigba 97, Walker 96, Bijan 95, Chase 91, Bowers 66, Josh Allen 66, Burrow 46, Trevor Lawrence 21. The scale is steep on purpose, because that is how the market behaves: two mid-tier players rarely fetch one star. Every suggestion (Browse, Buy, Sell, Trades to target) requires the value you send to be within range of what you get back (85–135% in Best for you; 90–120% plus a 10% consolidation premium in the fair groups). Players outside FantasyCalc’s list get an estimate from this tool’s own value scaled to the market (shown with ≈). If your league is half-PPR or Superflex, give me the FantasyCalc link with those settings and I will reload it.
  • Model value (0–100). Each skill player’s value is his rest-of-season points over a replacement starter (Weeks 10–17, playoff weeks weighted 1.5× when that option is on), using the projection basis in force. Week 10 is multiplied by his chance to play (questionable 75%, doubtful 20%, out 0%) and weeks he is expected to miss count as zero; on top of that, a player with a multi-week timeline loses a further 60% of the share of remaining weeks he misses (out through Week 14 means 5 of 8 weeks missed, so the remaining value is cut another 38%) to reflect setback risk and rust on return; the total is then tilted up to ±7.5% for recent form against his season average and discounted 5% (questionable) or 10% (doubtful). Scores are scaled so the top player is 100 (a 0.7 power keeps mid-tier players from bunching near zero). Quarterback values are halved in leagues without a Superflex slot, since a one-QB league has plenty of replacements. Lineup-based trade grades also use the Week 10 chance to play and the out timelines.
  • Standings and season adjustment. Where you enter records and points, a team’s expected weekly score is 60% lineup projection and 40% actual points per game; this drives the power rankings, the projected final record (current record plus the win chances of remaining scheduled games) and the season-adjusted win chances shown with matchups. Margin per game is points for minus points against.
  • Trades to target (My lineup). Weak spots are starters who project below a typical starter at that position for your league size (the average of the top 12-per-slot players by rest-of-season projection). Targets are players who would fill a weak spot, paired with the offer from your roster that comes closest to even on trade value (you send 90–125% of what you receive, plus a 10% premium on 2-for-1 packages because the other side has to consolidate) while still raising your projected best lineup by at least 0.4 a week. Without league rosters the pool is every player; with them, only rostered players, with owners shown. Buy and Sell on the Trades tab fall back to the same value matching when no rosters are loaded.
  • Suggested trades. Buy a player: pick someone on a leaguemate’s roster and the tool tries every package of one to three of your players (and 2-for-2 deals) for him. Sell a player: pick one of yours and it tries one or two players back from every team, or him plus one of yours for a bigger piece. Browse: every 1-for-1 swap between your roster and each leaguemate (plus 2-for-1 deals when one team is chosen) is scored by the change in each side’s projected best lineup per week. “Fair for both” keeps the other team within 0.3 a week; “Best for you” allows up to 1.5 a week of cost to them. Kickers and defenses are left out.
  • Where your data lives. Everything you enter is kept in this browser’s storage for this page and, when you are signed in on claude.ai, synced privately to your account so it survives page updates and follows you across devices (the badge in the header shows the sync state). Nothing is sent to Anthropic’s model or anyone else. Use Backup and restore under Rosters to copy your data out or paste it back in.
  • Your own lineup. “Set my own lineup” lets you fill each slot by hand; the table shows your total against the best-projected lineup, and saved combinations are re-scored whenever scoring, projection basis or injuries change. In head-to-head you can pick any of your lineups (best, set, or a saved combination) and set the opponent’s actual starters; the options table ranks your lineups by win chance against that lineup. Trades and power rankings always use best-projected lineups so comparisons stay apples to apples.
  • League schedule. Pairings are yours to enter for Weeks 10–17 (auto-fill pairs the remaining teams at random). Each matchup is scored with the teams’ best-projected lineups for that week, or the lineup you set for that team and week; future weeks use the projection basis plus that week’s matchup, with no injury or weather information yet. Win chances use the same rough margin-and-variance estimate as Head-to-head.
  • League and head-to-head. Leaguemate rosters live on the League tab. The head-to-head view builds each team’s best Week 10 lineup with the same model as yours, and the win chance is a rough estimate from the projected margin assuming typical week-to-week swings (about 45% of each starter’s projection). Power rankings use projected best-lineup points through Week 17.
  • Garbage time. Every 2026 play (nflverse play-by-play, through Week 9) is scored to the passer, rusher and receiver in PPR and tagged garbage time when it came in the second half with the live win probability under 5% or over 95%, or in the 4th quarter with a 17-point or larger gap. About 16% of plays qualify. A player’s garbage share is his garbage-time points over his total; competitive points per game excludes them. Players with 25% or more of at least 30 points in garbage time carry a GT tag. Reconstructed totals match official weekly scores within 0.2 points for 96% of player-weeks (laterals, two-point conversions and return touchdowns are the gaps).
  • Games already played. Once a Week 10 game is final (every game but Monday night’s Chargers–Ravens), each player from it shows his actual fantasy points in place of a projection, tagged FINAL, and those points flow into lineup totals, head-to-head, trade grades and the roster tables. His season average, touches, snaps, recent form and garbage-time splits are updated with the game as well (nflverse, Monday morning). Team defenses are scored the same way, using ESPN’s default D/ST rules.
  • Recent form. Weekly game logs for every player come from nflverse (through Week 9), with snap counts from the same source. The recent-form projection takes a player’s last 3 games, weights them 50/30/20 with the most recent first, divides each by how friendly that week’s defense is to his position (so a big game against a soft defense counts for less), then applies this week’s matchup, game environment, weather and injury adjustments. It does not use the usage tilt, since recent usage is already in the numbers. Backfield share and touches now use exact nflverse carries and catches. The Projection basis setting chooses which projection drives lineups, ranks, trades and head-to-head; the blend is 60% recent and 40% season. Enter your app’s projections in the roster-by-stat panel to see where they differ.
  • Route estimates. Routes run are not in public box scores; Next Gen Stats publishes them for about 95 receivers (through Week 8). For everyone else, routes per dropback is estimated from snap share: receivers at 95% of their snap share, tight ends at 68%, marked ≈. The usage tilt uses the estimate the same way.
  • Usage tilt. Running backs: projections move up to ±5% with backfield share (share of the team’s running-back carries among backs with 20 or more this season, so a lone back caps near 92%; centred on 55%) and up to ±3% with goal-line share (share of the team’s carries inside the 5, centred on 50%, once a back has at least 3 such carries). Receivers and tight ends: up to −4% / +2% with routes per team dropback (centred on 0.80). These tilt a season average toward or away from secure usage; they do not replace it.
  • Touches. Touches per game is season carries plus season receptions (FantasyData, through Week 9) divided by games played. Receivers and tight ends count receptions only. For a few deep backs whose receptions fall below the published list, catches are estimated from targets at an 82% catch rate, and for players without a games-played figure the team’s games are used; those cases show ≈. Targets per game come from targets per route and routes (Next Gen Stats, through Week 8).
  • Player list. About 510 players are preloaded: every QB, RB, WR and TE with a 2026 stat line (Edgehalla, PPR, through Week 9), all 37 kickers and all 32 team defenses. Receivers, quarterbacks and the top backs and tight ends use real 2026 season averages (FantasyTools, FF Beacon, Stat Sniper, Razzball). Only quarterbacks who have not played yet (McCord, Beck) are estimates, tagged “Estimate.” Players with one or two games have noisy averages. Anyone else can be added by name, team and position (or pasted in bulk). If you skip points per game, an average for the position is used and the player is tagged “Estimate.”
  • Kickers and defenses. A kicker’s projection scales his season average by his team’s Vegas implied points this week (with a wind or rain cut); a defense’s projection scales by the opposing offense’s implied points, lower being better. Future weeks use the season average. Neither has a defense-vs-position grade or a schedule rank. Kicker season averages and Week 10 results are scored from nflverse kicking lines through Week 10 (3, 4 or 5 points by field-goal distance, 1 per extra point, −1 per miss); team defenses are scored from official team box-score lines (nflverse) with your ESPN league’s D/ST rules through Week 10: 1 per sack, 2 per interception, fumble recovery, safety or blocked kick, 6 per defensive or return touchdown, 2 per two-point return, points-allowed brackets (0 = 5, 1–6 = 4, 7–13 = 3, 14–17 = 1, 18–27 = 0, 28–34 = −1, 35–45 = −3, 46+ = −5) and yards-allowed brackets (under 100 = 5, 100–199 = 3, 200–299 = 2, 300–349 = 0, 350–399 = −1, 400–449 = −3, 450–499 = −5, 500–549 = −6, 550+ = −7); points allowed exclude touchdowns scored by the opponent’s defense or return units.
